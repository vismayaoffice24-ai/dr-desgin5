Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD)

A comprehensive patient guide to causes, stages, warning signs, and preservation strategies.

What is Chronic Kidney Disease?

Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) is defined as persistent structural or functional damage to the kidneys present for more than three months. Your kidneys filter waste products and excess fluid from your bloodstream, which are subsequently excreted in urine.

When CKD progresses unchecked, hazardous levels of electrolytes, metabolic wastes, and fluid accumulate in the body, leading to systemic complications affecting the heart, bones, and blood vessels.

Common Symptoms & Warning Signs:

• Early Stages: Frequently completely asymptomatic ("the silent disease").
• Fluid Retention: Swelling in the ankles, feet, legs, or puffiness around eyes upon waking.
• Urinary Changes: Frothy or foamy urine, waking up multiple times at night to urinate (nocturia).
• General Fatigue: Persistent weakness, reduced stamina, pale complexion due to renal anemia.
• Uremic Symptoms: Decreased appetite, metallic taste in food, nausea, itchy dry skin.

What Causes Chronic Kidney Disease?

The two leading causes account for over 70% of all CKD cases worldwide:

  • Diabetes Mellitus (Diabetic Nephropathy): Chronic high blood sugar damages the microvasculature of the glomeruli.
  • High Blood Pressure (Hypertensive Nephrosclerosis): Long-standing hypertension thickens and hardens renal arteries.
  • Glomerulonephritis: Autoimmune and inflammatory diseases of the filtering units (such as IgA nephropathy or Lupus).
  • Polycystic Kidney Disease (PKD): A genetic condition causing fluid-filled cysts to progressively displace healthy renal tissue.
  • Prolonged Obstruction: Enlarged prostate, recurrent kidney stones, or strictures.
  • Analgesic Nephropathy: Unchecked daily use of NSAID painkillers (ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac).

How is CKD Treated by a Nephrologist?

While destroyed nephrons cannot be regenerated, active medical therapy can stabilize remaining functional tissue:

  1. Blood Pressure Target: Maintaining blood pressure consistently below 130/80 mmHg using ACE inhibitors or ARBs.
  2. Proteinuria Reduction: Lowering albumin loss through modern SGLT2 inhibitors and non-steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists.
  3. Renal-Friendly Nutrition: Restricting dietary sodium (<2000 mg/day), moderating dietary protein to prevent hyperfiltration, and managing potassium and phosphorus intake.
  4. Managing Complications: Correcting metabolic acidosis with sodium bicarbonate, treating anemia with erythropoietin, and balancing calcium/Vitamin D.
