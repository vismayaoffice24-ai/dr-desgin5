Kidney Stones (Nephrolithiasis)

Understanding causes, recognizing renal colic symptoms, and preventing painful recurrences through metabolic nephrology care.

What Are Kidney Stones?

Kidney stones (nephrolithiasis or renal calculi) are solid mineral and acid salt deposits that crystallize inside the renal calyces and renal pelvis. Stones can vary in size from microscopic grains of sand to several centimeters in diameter.

While small stones can pass silently through the ureter and bladder, larger stones can obstruct the flow of urine, causing severe spasming pain (renal colic), back pressure into the kidney (hydronephrosis), and possible bacterial infection.

Emergency Red Flag Signs (Seek Immediate Hospital Care):

• Inability to urinate or very low urine output (complete obstruction).
• Severe flank pain accompanied by high fever and shaking chills (infected kidney stone / urosepsis).
• Intractable nausea and vomiting that prevents keeping oral fluids down.
• Presence of solitary kidney with sudden severe flank pain.

Classic Symptoms of Renal Stones

  • Excruciating Flank Pain: Intense cramping pain in the back and side beneath the ribs that radiates down to the lower abdomen, groin, or inner thigh.
  • Hematuria: Urine that appears pink, red, or rusty brown due to mechanical abrasion of ureteral walls.
  • Dysuria & Urgency: Painful or burning urination and feeling a constant urge to urinate.
  • Cloudy or Foul-Smelling Urine: Indicating potential underlying urinary tract infection.

Why Do Stones Recur?

Without metabolic diagnosis and correction, stone recurrence exceeds 50% within 5 to 7 years. Common triggers include:

  • Chronic dehydration and low daily urine volume.
  • High dietary intake of sodium, which forces kidneys to excrete higher amounts of calcium.
  • Excessive animal protein and purine-rich foods that lower urinary pH and elevate uric acid levels.
  • Low levels of urinary citrate (hypocitraturia)—citrate is the body's natural chemical inhibitor of crystal formation.
  • Hyperparathyroidism or primary hyperoxaluria.
