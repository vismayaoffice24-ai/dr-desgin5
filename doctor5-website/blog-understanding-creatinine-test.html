Understanding Your Creatinine & eGFR Report

By Dr. Aishwarya Lal, Consultant Nephrologist • October 2026 • 5 min read

Demystifying Renal Lab Numbers

Receiving an abnormal blood test report stating that your serum creatinine is elevated can create intense anxiety. Many patients immediately assume they are facing imminent dialysis. In reality, understanding what creatinine is, why it fluctuates, and how it correlates with estimated Glomerular Filtration Rate (eGFR) provides clarity and peace of mind.

What is Serum Creatinine?

Creatinine is a natural waste byproduct generated from the normal wear and tear of your muscles. Healthy kidneys filter almost all creatinine out of the blood and pass it out through urine. If kidneys become impaired, creatinine remains in the bloodstream, causing blood levels to rise.

Normal Serum Creatinine Reference Ranges:

• Adult Men: Approximately 0.7 to 1.3 mg/dL (men naturally have higher muscle mass).
• Adult Women: Approximately 0.5 to 1.1 mg/dL.
Note: A small muscular athlete may have a creatinine of 1.4 mg/dL with healthy kidneys, whereas a frail elderly woman with a creatinine of 1.1 mg/dL may actually have significant kidney impairment!

Why eGFR is the More Accurate Measure

To overcome variations in muscle mass, age, and gender, nephrologists calculate the estimated Glomerular Filtration Rate (eGFR). Think of eGFR roughly as the percentage of kidney function you have working:

  • eGFR 90+: Normal kidney function (unless there is protein in urine).
  • eGFR 60 to 89: Mild decline. Common in aging adults; often harmless if stable.
  • eGFR 30 to 59: Moderate kidney disease (Stage 3). Requires active nephrology management to protect function.
  • eGFR 15 to 29: Severe reduction (Stage 4). Requires close monitoring and vascular access planning.
  • eGFR Below 15: Kidney failure (Stage 5). Discussions on dialysis or transplant options.

Why Can Creatinine Temporarily Fluctuate?

Creatinine can jump higher without permanent kidney failure due to:

  • Severe dehydration or fasting before the blood draw.
  • Consuming a very large meal of red meat or cooked animal protein the night before.
  • Heavy resistance weightlifting or intense marathons.
  • Recent use of painkillers (NSAIDs) or certain antibiotics (trimethoprim).
  • Starting ACE inhibitors or ARBs (a harmless 15–20% rise is expected due to lowering intraglomerular pressure).
