Common Inquiries

Frequently Asked Questions

Find clear, straightforward answers to the most common questions patients ask Dr. Aishwarya Lal.

When should I see a kidney specialist (nephrologist)?

You should consult a nephrologist if you notice persistent swelling of your ankles, feet, or face; foamy or bubbly urine; tea-colored urine; blood tests showing rising serum creatinine (>1.2 mg/dL for women, >1.4 mg/dL for men); eGFR below 60 mL/min/1.73m²; long-standing diabetes (>5 years); blood pressure that is difficult to control with multiple medicines; or recurrent kidney stones.

What should I bring to my first visit with Dr. Aishwarya Lal?

Please bring all past blood and urine test reports (specifically serum creatinine, urea, electrolytes, complete blood count, and urine routine/albumin-to-creatinine ratio), previous abdominal ultrasounds or CT scans, discharge summaries from any hospital admissions, and a complete, up-to-date list of all prescription medications, pain relievers, and supplements you are currently taking.

Does high creatinine always mean permanent kidney disease or failure?

No, not always. A sudden increase in creatinine can be caused by reversible Acute Kidney Injury (AKI) due to dehydration, heat exhaustion, severe infection, or certain medications (such as NSAID painkillers or ACE inhibitors during dehydration). Once fluid balance is restored and offending drugs are paused, creatinine often returns to normal. Even in chronic kidney disease, early intervention stabilizes numbers and delays progression for decades.

How can I protect my kidneys every single day?

Key daily practices include: maintaining optimal hydration (around 2 to 2.5 liters of clean water daily, unless fluid-restricted), keeping blood pressure strictly below 130/80 mmHg, managing blood sugar levels, limiting daily salt intake to under 5 grams (1 level teaspoon), avoiding over-the-counter NSAID painkillers (like ibuprofen, naproxen, and diclofenac), avoiding unverified herbal concoctions, quitting smoking, and getting annual blood and urine tests if you have diabetes or hypertension.

What is dialysis and can it be delayed?

Dialysis is an artificial filtering therapy that removes metabolic toxins and excess fluid when kidneys lose approximately 85% to 90% of their filtering ability (Stage 5 CKD). With early nephrology care, modern medications (such as SGLT2 inhibitors and ARBs), and strict blood pressure control, many patients can delay the need for dialysis by years or decades.

Can I schedule a WhatsApp or remote report consultation?

Yes. Dr. Aishwarya Lal offers structured remote report evaluations and second opinions via WhatsApp for patients who have recent blood/urine reports or wish to review their medication plan from home. However, acute emergencies like sudden inability to urinate or severe breathlessness require immediate emergency department care.

What dietary restrictions will I need to follow?

Dietary needs depend on your specific stage of kidney disease. In early stages, dietary focus is primarily on sodium moderation and adequate hydration. In advanced stages (Stage 4 or 5), potassium and phosphorus restriction may be required based on your blood lab values. Dr. Aishwarya provides customized, practical nutritional plans rather than imposing unscientific, harsh blanket bans.
